What you are about to eat, and why it exists

Three dishes, each with the story Koreans tell about it.

i.

Seolleongtang

설렁탕 · ox-bone soup
restaurant photo
A story often told

Each spring, Joseon kings performed a farming rite at Seonnongdan, an altar in eastern Seoul. A popular tale says the ox offered there was simmered for hours in one great pot so the crowd could share it, and that the soup took its name from the altar. Historians still debate the name — the bowl in front of you is not debated.

How to eat — It arrives unsalted. Add salt and spring onion yourself, then drop in rice.

ii.

Sinseollo

신선로 · royal hot pot
restaurant photo
Court cuisine

A dish of the royal table, named after the brass vessel it is served in: a ring-shaped pot around a central chimney that once held burning charcoal. Its older name, yeolguja-tang, is usually translated as “a soup that delights the mouth.” Meat, fish and vegetables are laid in coloured rings, the way a painter arranges a palette.

How to eat — Share from the centre outward; ladle broth over each piece.

iii.

Bindaetteok

빈대떡 · mung bean pancake
restaurant photo
Origins disputed

Ground mung beans fried thick and crisp. Nobody agrees where the name comes from; one widely repeated theory links it to binja-tteok, “the poor man’s pancake.” Today it is the classic partner of makgeolli on a rainy evening.

How to eat — Tear, dip in soy sauce with onion, and order a bowl of makgeolli.
